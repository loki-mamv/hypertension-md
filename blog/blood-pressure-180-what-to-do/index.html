What Should You Do If Your Blood Pressure Reading Is 180 or Higher?

The short answer: a reading of 180/120 mm Hg or higher is a hypertensive crisis, and the next step depends on one question: do you have symptoms? If the high reading comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, weakness or numbness, vision change, trouble speaking, or a severe headache, call 911. If you feel fine, sit quietly for five minutes and take the reading again. If it is still 180/120 or higher, seek medical care promptly. Do not take an extra dose of medication on your own to force the number down.

That distinction, symptoms or no symptoms, is the whole decision. Here is why it matters and what happens next in each case.

What does a reading of 180 actually mean?

The American Heart Association puts a reading of 180/120 mm Hg or higher in its own category, above stage 2 hypertension, and calls it a hypertensive crisis (AHA, Understanding Blood Pressure Readings). Either number crossing that line counts. A reading of 184/96 is a crisis. So is 172/124.

For context, the rest of the scale under the 2025 AHA/ACC high blood pressure guideline looks like this:

  • Normal: below 120 systolic and below 80 diastolic
  • Elevated: 120 to 129 systolic and below 80 diastolic
  • Stage 1: 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic
  • Stage 2: 140 or higher systolic or 90 or higher diastolic
  • Crisis: 180 or higher systolic or 120 or higher diastolic

The guideline keeps 130/80 mm Hg as both the diagnostic threshold and the general treatment target for adults (2025 High Blood Pressure Guideline-at-a-Glance, JACC). A single reading at 180 does not mean your usual blood pressure is 180. It means this reading needs a response now.

Urgency versus emergency: the only question that matters

Clinicians split a hypertensive crisis into two, and the split is based on whether organs are being damaged right now rather than on how big the number is.

Hypertensive emergency means 180/120 or higher plus signs that the heart, brain, kidneys, or eyes are under acute strain. This is a call-911 situation. The AHA lists the warning signs to act on (AHA, When to Call 911):

  • Chest pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • Back pain
  • Numbness or weakness, especially on one side
  • Change in vision
  • Difficulty speaking
  • Severe headache

Hypertensive urgency means the same numbers with none of those symptoms. It is serious but not immediately organ-threatening, and it is usually handled by adjusting medication over days rather than by an ambulance ride (Cleveland Clinic, Hypertensive Crisis).

Do not try to decide which one you have by how bad the number looks. Decide by the symptom list.

What to do in the next ten minutes

If you have any of the symptoms above, stop reading and call 911. If you do not, work through this:

  1. Sit still for five minutes. Feet flat on the floor, back supported, arm resting at heart level, no talking. Do not measure right after climbing stairs, arguing, or drinking coffee.
  2. Take the reading again on the same arm with a properly sized cuff placed on bare skin.
  3. If it has come down, record both numbers and call your usual clinician during office hours. Tell them exactly what you saw and follow their guidance.
  4. If it is still 180/120 or higher, the AHA advice is to seek medical care rather than wait it out. Call your clinician's office or on-call line now; if you cannot reach anyone, go to urgent care or an emergency department.
  5. Either way, write it down. Time, both numbers, which arm, what you were doing beforehand, and any medication you missed.

Why you should not force the number down fast

This is the part most home advice gets backwards. In hypertensive urgency, dropping blood pressure quickly is not the goal and can cause harm.

Blood vessels in people with chronic hypertension adapt to operating at a higher pressure. Cutting that pressure abruptly can under-perfuse the brain, heart, and kidneys, which is why clinicians lower it gradually over hours to days rather than minutes (Hypertensive Crisis, StatPearls, NCBI Bookshelf). Even in a true emergency, the target in the first hour is a controlled partial reduction, not normalization.

So: no extra doses on your own, no borrowing a family member's pills, no stacking medications hoping to see a better number in twenty minutes. The exception is a written action plan your own clinician gave you that names a specific reading and a specific instruction. Follow that.

What makes a reading spike?

Once the immediate situation is handled, the useful question is what pushed it there. Common contributors:

  • Missed doses. The single most frequent cause of a sudden jump. Abruptly stopping some medications, particularly beta blockers and clonidine, can cause rebound increases.
  • NSAIDs. Regular ibuprofen or naproxen raises blood pressure and blunts several antihypertensives.
  • Decongestants. Pseudoephedrine and phenylephrine are in a lot of cold and allergy products.
  • Salt load. A single very high sodium meal can move readings measurably.
  • Untreated sleep apnea. A leading cause of blood pressure that resists treatment, and it often shows up first as high morning readings.
  • Pain, acute anxiety, or a panic episode. These raise pressure temporarily and genuinely. That does not make the reading fake, and it does not make it dangerous on its own. More on that at anxiety.md.
  • Measurement error. A cuff that is too small, an unsupported arm, or talking during the reading all inflate the result.

If the number is only high in the clinic and normal at home, that pattern has a name and a workup of its own. See our guide to white coat hypertension.

What happens if you do go in?

For a hypertensive emergency, expect blood tests for kidney function, an ECG, and depending on symptoms a chest X-ray or CT of the head, plus intravenous medication in a monitored setting so the pressure comes down in a controlled way.

For urgency without symptoms, the visit is usually far less dramatic: repeat measurements, a check for the triggers above, and a change to your oral regimen with follow-up in days. Many people are surprised that they are not admitted. That is the correct outcome, not a dismissal.

Preventing the next one

A crisis reading is a signal that day-to-day control is not where it should be. The follow-up work is ordinary and effective: confirm the diagnosis with home readings taken correctly, review every medication including over-the-counter products, address adherence honestly, get screened for sleep apnea if you snore or wake unrefreshed, and revisit the regimen. The bottom line

A reading of 180/120 or higher is a real event, not a glitch. With symptoms, it is an emergency and 911 is the right call. Without symptoms, rest, recheck, and if it stays there, get seen promptly. The one thing not to do is treat it yourself with extra medication, because the speed of the drop can hurt you more than the number itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a blood pressure of 180 an emergency?

It depends entirely on symptoms. The American Heart Association defines a reading of 180/120 mm Hg or higher as a hypertensive crisis. If it comes with chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, vision change, difficulty speaking, or severe headache, that is a hypertensive emergency and you should call 911. If you feel completely normal, it is a hypertensive urgency: rest five minutes, take a second reading, and if it is still 180/120 or higher, seek medical care promptly.

Should I take an extra blood pressure pill to bring it down?

Not without being told to. Rapidly dropping blood pressure can reduce blood flow to the brain, heart, and kidneys and cause harm. Doubling your own dose or taking someone else's medication is not a safe home remedy. Call your prescriber and describe the readings. If you have a written action plan from your clinician that tells you what to take at a specific number, follow that plan.

What if the reading was high once and normal after?

One high reading is not a diagnosis. Cuff size, arm position, a full bladder, caffeine, pain, talking during the measurement, and stress all inflate readings. If the repeat reading drops, call your usual clinician during office hours, tell them what happened, and follow their guidance. Bring your home monitor to the visit so it can be checked against theirs.

Does a headache mean my blood pressure is dangerously high?

Not usually. Most high blood pressure produces no symptoms at all, which is why it is called silent. But a sudden severe headache alongside a reading of 180/120 or higher is one of the symptoms that turns a crisis into an emergency, particularly when it comes with vision change, confusion, or trouble speaking. Do not talk yourself out of calling 911 in that combination.

What counts as high blood pressure now?

Under the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, normal is below 120/80 mm Hg, elevated is 120 to 129 systolic with diastolic under 80, stage 1 hypertension is 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic, and stage 2 is 140 or higher systolic or 90 or higher diastolic. The general treatment goal is below 130/80 mm Hg.
