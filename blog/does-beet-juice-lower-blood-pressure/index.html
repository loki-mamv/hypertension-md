Does Beet Juice Lower Blood Pressure?

November 15, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida
Table of Contents

The short answer: Yes, modestly, and mostly the top number. A 2024 meta-analysis of 11 trials in people with hypertension found nitrate-rich beet juice lowered clinic systolic pressure by about 5 mm Hg versus placebo. The certainty was low, and diastolic and 24-hour readings did not change. It does not replace prescribed medication.

How could beet juice lower blood pressure?

The active part is inorganic nitrate. Beets are rich in it, and so are green leafy vegetables. Your body turns some of that nitrate into nitric oxide, a gas that relaxes blood vessel walls.

The route is unusual. It runs through your mouth:

  1. You drink beet juice or eat nitrate-rich vegetables. Nitrate is absorbed into the blood.
  2. Your salivary glands pull nitrate out of the blood and concentrate it in saliva.
  3. Bacteria in the mouth convert nitrate to nitrite.
  4. You swallow the nitrite. Some of it becomes nitric oxide in the blood and tissues, which widens blood vessels.

A 2013 study by Kapil and colleagues described this pathway and showed why step 3 matters. In 19 healthy adults, a week of antiseptic mouthwash cut oral nitrite production by 90% and plasma nitrite by 25%. Systolic and diastolic pressure rose by about 2 to 3.5 mm Hg within one day.

What do the meta-analyses show?

Pooled trials agree on a small drop in systolic pressure. They agree less on diastolic pressure and on lasting effects.

  • 2013, J Nutr. Siervo and colleagues pooled 16 short crossover trials with 254 people, mostly lasting 2 hours to 15 days. Systolic pressure fell 4.4 mm Hg. The 1.1 mm Hg diastolic drop was not significant. Higher nitrate doses were linked to larger systolic drops.
  • 2017, J Hypertens. An update by the same group looked at 13 trials lasting 1 to 6 weeks. Systolic fell 4.1 mm Hg and diastolic 2.0 mm Hg. But the effect showed up only in clinic readings, not in 24-hour or home monitoring.
  • 2022, Front Nutr. A review of 7 trials in people with hypertension (218 people) found systolic fell about 5 mm Hg. Diastolic did not change significantly.
  • 2024, Nutr Metab Cardiovasc Dis. The 11-trial review in the short answer found a 5.3 mm Hg systolic drop, with no sign that the effect wore off. The authors rated certainty as low.

A 4 to 5 mm Hg drop in systolic pressure is real but small. None of these reviews tested beet juice as a replacement for medication.

Does it work if you already take blood pressure medication?

The results are mixed. Two well-known trials point in different directions.

In a 2015 trial in Hypertension, Kapil and colleagues gave 68 people with hypertension 250 mL of beet juice daily for 4 weeks. Half were on medication, and half were not. Compared with nitrate-free beet juice, clinic pressure fell 7.7/2.4 mm Hg. Home pressure fell 8.1/3.8 mm Hg, and 24-hour pressure fell 7.7/5.2 mm Hg.

A 2015 trial in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found the opposite in 27 people with treated hypertension. One week of nitrate-rich beet juice raised plasma nitrate and nitrite about threefold. But home and 24-hour blood pressure did not differ from placebo.

Pills that deliver nitrate without the beet do not settle the question either. A 2026 meta-analysis of 6 sodium nitrate trials (181 adults, mostly over 50) found no significant drop in systolic or diastolic pressure. The authors said more trials are needed.

How much beet juice did the trials use?

This is what trials gave, not a recommendation. The 2022 review covered daily amounts of 70 to 250 mL of beet juice, taken for 3 to 60 days. The 2024 review described daily nitrate intakes of 200 to 800 mg from beet juice. Nitrate content varies a lot between brands and between home-made juices, so a glass of juice is not a fixed dose.

Most trials compared nitrate-rich juice with juice that had its nitrate removed. That design shows the nitrate is doing the work. It also means eating more nitrate-rich vegetables is a reasonable food-first route. A vegetable-heavy pattern such as the DASH diet already does this. If you track the sugar and calories that juice adds, calories.md can help you fit it into your day.

Is beet juice safe, and who should be careful?

For most people, beet juice is a food, and the 2015 Kapil trial reported it was well tolerated. A few points are worth knowing:

  • Pink or red urine. MedlinePlus lists beets as a cause of pink or red urine. This is harmless, but do not assume red urine is beets if you have not eaten any.
  • Kidney stones. The National Kidney Foundation names beets among foods very high in oxalate. If you have had calcium oxalate stones with high urine oxalate, ask your clinician before drinking it daily.
  • Erectile dysfunction drugs. The Viagra (sildenafil) label contraindicates "organic nitrates or organic nitrites in any form," such as nitroglycerin. Beet nitrate is inorganic, and the label does not mention it. Still, both lower pressure, so tell your prescriber if you plan to use concentrated beet products. Our post on blood pressure medications and erectile dysfunction covers the nitrate-drug rule.
  • Your prescription. Do not stop or cut back blood pressure medication because you started beet juice. Any change belongs with your prescriber.

How can you tell if it works for you?

If you and your clinician agree to try it, a simple home check helps. This plan follows how the trials measured results:

  1. Take home readings for one week first, at the same times each day. This is your baseline.
  2. Keep your medication, salt intake, and exercise the same during the test.
  3. Skip antiseptic mouthwash if your dentist agrees, since it blunted the nitrate pathway in the 2013 study.
  4. Drink the juice daily for about 4 weeks, the length of the largest positive trial.
  5. Compare your weekly averages. Single readings vary, so do not judge by one number.
  6. Share the log with your clinician.

Other food steps have similar or better evidence. See our guides on whether magnesium lowers blood pressure and the DASH diet.

The bottom line

Nitrate-rich beet juice lowers systolic blood pressure by about 4 to 5 mm Hg on average in short trials. The effect on diastolic pressure is small or absent. Results in people already on medication are mixed, and the certainty of evidence is low. It is a reasonable food add-on for some people, not a substitute for treatment. Keep taking your prescribed medication, and check with your clinician if you have kidney stones or take erectile dysfunction drugs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How fast does beet juice lower blood pressure?

Trials in the 2013 review lasted from 2 hours to 15 days, and later trials ran 1 to 6 weeks. The largest positive trial in people with hypertension lasted 4 weeks with no sign the effect faded.

Are beet powders or capsules the same as beet juice?

Not always. What matters is the nitrate content, and labels often do not state it. A 2026 review of sodium nitrate pills found no significant blood pressure drop, so results from beet juice trials may not carry over to every product.

Does mouthwash cancel out beet juice?

It may weaken it. In a 2013 study, one week of antiseptic mouthwash cut oral nitrite production by 90% and raised blood pressure by 2 to 3.5 mm Hg in healthy adults. Ask your dentist before stopping a mouthwash you were told to use.

Can I eat whole beets or leafy greens instead of juice?

Yes, beets and green leafy vegetables also supply nitrate. The amount per serving varies, and whole-food intake was not what most juice trials tested. A vegetable-rich eating pattern like DASH has strong evidence for lowering blood pressure.

Is red urine after beets a sign of a problem?

Usually not. MedlinePlus lists beets as a common cause of pink or red urine. If you see red urine without eating beets or other red foods, contact your clinician because it can be blood.
