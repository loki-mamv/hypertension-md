What Happens if You Miss a Dose of Blood Pressure Medication?

October 16, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: For most common blood pressure pills, the official instruction is simple: take the missed dose when you remember, unless it is almost time for the next one. Then skip it and never take a double dose. One late or missed pill usually causes no harm. The real risks come from stopping beta blockers such as atenolol and metoprolol, or clonidine, all at once.

What do the drug guides say to do after a missed dose?

MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's consumer drug guide, gives almost the same rule for most blood pressure medicines. Here is what each page says:

  • Lisinopril (an ACE inhibitor): take it as soon as you remember. If it is almost time for the next dose, skip the missed one.
  • Losartan (an ARB): the same rule as lisinopril.
  • Hydrochlorothiazide (a thiazide diuretic): the same rule.
  • Amlodipine (a calcium channel blocker): take it when you remember, but skip it if your next dose is less than 12 hours away.
  • Metoprolol (a beta blocker): MedlinePlus says to skip the missed dose and continue your regular schedule.

Every one of these pages says the same thing at the end: do not take a double dose to make up for a missed one. Two doses close together can push your pressure too low. That can cause dizziness or a fall, especially when you stand up. Our guide to what blood pressure is too low explains those symptoms.

Your own instructions may differ. Extended-release forms, combination pills, and twice-daily schedules can change the timing. The pharmacy leaflet that came with your prescription is the best quick reference. A pharmacist can answer the question by phone in a minute.

Is one missed dose dangerous?

For most people, no. Blood pressure medicine works by keeping pressure down over weeks and years. One missed pill does not undo that. Your reading may be a bit higher that day, and it should settle once you are back on schedule.

All five MedlinePlus pages above also say to keep taking the drug even if you feel well. High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, so feeling fine is not a sign that you no longer need the pill. The harm from high blood pressure builds slowly, which is why steady daily use matters more than any single dose.

If you missed a dose and want reassurance, check your pressure at home with a validated cuff, seated and rested.

Why does it matter how long each drug lasts?

A drug's half-life is the time it takes for half of it to leave your blood. A long half-life means a missed dose leaves more drug still working. The FDA-approved labels give these figures:

  • Amlodipine: a terminal elimination half-life of about 30 to 50 hours, per the amlodipine label. It takes 7 to 8 days of daily dosing to reach steady levels. This is one reason a single missed amlodipine dose tends to matter less.
  • Lisinopril: an effective half-life of 12 hours with repeated dosing, per the lisinopril label.
  • Losartan: about 2 hours for losartan itself and 6 to 9 hours for its active metabolite, per the losartan label.
  • Hydrochlorothiazide: between 5.6 and 14.8 hours, per the hydrochlorothiazide label.
  • Metoprolol: about 3 to 4 hours for the immediate-release tartrate form, per the metoprolol tartrate label, and up to 7 to 9 hours in people who break it down slowly.
  • Clonidine: 12 to 16 hours, per the clonidine label, and up to 41 hours with severe kidney disease.

Half-life is not the whole story. Many drugs keep lowering pressure longer than their blood levels suggest. The atenolol label, for example, says its blood pressure effect lasts at least 24 hours even though its half-life is about 6 to 7 hours. Still, these numbers explain why the drug guides give slightly different timing rules.

Which blood pressure drugs can cause a rebound if you stop them?

A rebound is a sharp rise in blood pressure or heart strain after a drug stops suddenly. It is the main reason not to stop these medicines on your own. Two groups stand out.

Beta blockers. The atenolol label carries a boxed warning, the FDA's strongest, titled "Cessation of Therapy." It says severe worsening of angina, heart attacks, and dangerous heart rhythms have been reported after beta blockers were stopped suddenly. Because heart disease is common and often not yet found, the label advises against abrupt stopping even in people treated only for blood pressure.

Current metoprolol labels give the same warning, but as a standard warning rather than a boxed one. The metoprolol succinate extended-release label says to reduce the dose gradually over 1 to 2 weeks when stopping. It also warns patients not to interrupt therapy without their doctor's advice. MedlinePlus states that stopping metoprolol suddenly can cause serious heart problems.

Clonidine. The clonidine label warns that sudden stopping can cause nervousness, agitation, headache, and tremor, with a rapid rise in blood pressure. It notes rare reports of hypertensive encephalopathy, stroke, and death after withdrawal. The label tells doctors to reduce the dose over 2 to 4 days. It also notes that children, who often get vomiting illnesses, may be especially prone to blood pressure spikes when they suddenly cannot take a dose.

By contrast, the losartan label states there is no apparent rebound effect after abrupt withdrawal. That does not make stopping safe, since your pressure will drift back up. If you are thinking about stopping, read whether you can stop blood pressure medication and talk to your prescriber first.

What symptoms after a missed dose need urgent care?

Most people notice nothing after a missed dose. Get help right away if you have any of these, especially after missing several doses of a beta blocker or clonidine:

  • Chest pain or pressure
  • A pounding or very fast heartbeat
  • A severe headache, confusion, or vision changes
  • Weakness or numbness on one side, or trouble speaking
  • A home reading of 180/120 mm Hg or higher

Our page on what to do at 180 explains when a high reading is an emergency. If you are not sure what a new symptom means, symptom.md has plain guides to chest pain, headaches, and palpitations.

How common are missed doses, and how can you miss fewer?

Missed doses are very common. A CDC analysis of 18.5 million Medicare Part D users in 2014 found that 26.3% were nonadherent, meaning they had their pills less than 80% of the time (MMWR, 2016). The rate ranged from 16.9% for ARBs to 28.9% for diuretics. The CDC called nonadherence a leading cause of poor blood pressure control.

Simple habits help:

  • Take your pill at the same time each day, tied to a daily routine such as brushing your teeth.
  • Use a weekly pill box so you can see at a glance if you missed a dose.
  • Set a phone alarm.
  • Ask about once-daily or combination pills if a complex schedule is the problem.
  • Refill early, and ask about 90-day supplies.

If side effects are why you skip doses, say so. There are many other drug options, and a switch is often easy.

The bottom line

For lisinopril, losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and amlodipine, take a missed dose when you remember unless the next one is close. For metoprolol, MedlinePlus says to skip it. Never double up. One missed pill is rarely a problem. Stopping a beta blocker or clonidine suddenly can be, so taper only with your prescriber's plan. When in doubt, call your pharmacist.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I take my blood pressure pill if I remember it at night?

For most once-daily pills, MedlinePlus says to take it when you remember unless the next dose is close. For amlodipine, the cutoff it gives is 12 hours before the next dose. Check your own pharmacy leaflet or call a pharmacist if you are unsure.

What happens if I accidentally take two doses of my blood pressure medicine?

Your pressure may drop more than usual, which can cause dizziness or lightheadedness, especially when standing. Sit or lie down if you feel faint and avoid driving. Call your pharmacist or Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 for advice, and call 911 for fainting, chest pain, or trouble breathing.

Will my blood pressure spike if I miss one day of lisinopril or losartan?

Your reading may be somewhat higher, but a dangerous spike from one missed dose is unlikely. The losartan label reports no apparent rebound after abrupt withdrawal. Restart your normal schedule and check your pressure at home if you are worried.

How long can I safely go without my blood pressure medication?

There is no safe number of days that applies to everyone. Beta blockers and clonidine carry label warnings about sudden stopping, and other drugs simply stop working as levels fall. If you have run out, a pharmacist can often provide a short emergency supply while you reach your prescriber.

Is it okay to skip blood pressure medicine when I feel fine?

No. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so feeling well does not mean your pressure is controlled. MedlinePlus pages for these drugs all say to keep taking them even if you feel well.
