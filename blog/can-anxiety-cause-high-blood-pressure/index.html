Can Anxiety Cause High Blood Pressure?

Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Anxiety reliably pushes blood pressure up for minutes at a time, but that spike is not the same as hypertension. Whether anxiety causes lasting high blood pressure is less clear. A 2015 meta-analysis found anxious adults had a 55 percent higher risk of later hypertension, yet other large studies point the other way.

What happens to blood pressure during anxiety or a panic attack?

When you feel threatened, your body runs its fight-or-flight response. Your heart beats faster and your blood vessels tighten. Blood pressure goes up quickly. When the threat passes, it falls back toward your usual level.

Researchers measure this directly. In lab studies, people do a stressful task, such as mental arithmetic or public speaking, while a cuff records their pressure. The rise during the task is called stress reactivity. The return to baseline afterward is called recovery.

The rise is real, and it shows up on your monitor. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) lists being excited or nervous as one of the factors that can make a reading inaccurate. So a high number taken during a panic attack tells you about that moment. It does not tell you your true resting blood pressure.

Is a short spike the same as having hypertension?

No. Hypertension is a diagnosis based on pressure that stays high at rest. The NHLBI says a provider may diagnose it when readings are consistently high, confirmed by two or more readings at separate appointments.

The NHLBI categories, in mm Hg, are:

  • Normal: under 120 systolic and under 80 diastolic.
  • Elevated: 120 to 129 systolic and under 80 diastolic.
  • Stage 1: 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic.
  • Stage 2: 140 or higher systolic or 90 or higher diastolic.

A single reading of 150/95 during a panic attack does not put you in stage 2. An average of 150/95 across calm readings on different days does. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline keeps a treatment goal under 130/80 mm Hg for adults. Treatment decisions rest on your usual pressure, measured carefully over time.

Does anxiety cause long-term high blood pressure?

The evidence points in two directions. Here is what the main studies actually found.

Evidence for a link. A 2015 meta-analysis in Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment pooled 13 cross-sectional studies (151,389 people) and 8 prospective studies (80,146 people). In the cross-sectional studies, anxiety was linked to hypertension with an odds ratio of 1.18. In the prospective studies, which follow people over time, the adjusted hazard ratio was 1.55. That means about a 55 percent higher risk of developing hypertension. The authors flagged high variation between studies, with an I-squared near 85 percent.

Evidence for repeated spikes mattering. A 2010 meta-analysis in Hypertension reviewed 36 articles on lab stress reactivity. People whose pressure jumped more under acute mental stress tended to have worse heart health later, including new hypertension. The effect size was small (a correlation of about 0.09).

Evidence against a simple link. The Nord-Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT) in Norway points the other way. In a cross-sectional analysis of 60,799 adults, people with the lowest systolic pressure were more likely to report anxiety, not less. A follow-up of 36,530 adults over 11 years found that high anxiety and depression symptoms predicted lower systolic pressure later.

So the honest answer is that anxiety is associated with hypertension in many studies, but cause and effect are not proven. The studies differ in how they measure anxiety and blood pressure, which may explain the split.

Could high blood pressure cause anxiety instead?

Possibly, at least in part. A 1999 study in the American Journal of Medicine compared 351 primary care patients with hypertension against matched patients with normal pressure. Panic attacks in the past 6 months were more common in the hypertension group (17 percent versus 11 percent). Panic disorder was also more common (13 percent versus 8 percent).

The timing is the interesting part. In most patients, the hypertension diagnosis came before the panic attacks started. Being told you have a chronic heart risk, then watching the numbers, can itself feed anxiety. That loop is worth knowing about if checking your pressure makes you tense.

The American Heart Association looked at the bigger picture in a 2021 scientific statement on psychological health and the heart. It concluded there are clear associations between psychological health and cardiovascular disease. It also said there is increasing evidence of a causal link through biology and behavior. Behavior is a big part of it: stress can drive poor sleep, heavy drinking, smoking, and less exercise, which all affect blood pressure.

How does anxiety overlap with white coat hypertension?

White coat hypertension means your pressure is high in the clinic but normal elsewhere. Nervousness about the visit is one common driver. The CDC says as many as 1 in 3 people with a high reading at the doctor's office may have normal readings at other times.

The NHLBI notes that providers spot this pattern by comparing office readings with readings taken elsewhere. The reverse pattern, masked hypertension, is normal in the office but high at home. That is why home readings matter in both directions. Our guide to white coat hypertension covers the clinic side in detail. If anxiety affects your daily life beyond the doctor's office, anxiety.md covers symptoms and treatment options.

How do you get an accurate reading when you are anxious?

The goal is to measure your resting pressure, not your stress response. This checklist combines the CDC home measurement steps with what the anxiety research shows.

  1. Do not measure during a panic attack. The number will be high, and seeing it can make the anxiety worse. Wait until you feel calm again.
  2. Skip food and drink for 30 minutes first. The CDC lists this as a standard step.
  3. Empty your bladder. A full bladder raises the reading.
  4. Sit quietly for at least 5 minutes with your back supported, feet flat, and legs uncrossed.
  5. Rest your arm on a table so the cuff sits at chest height, on bare skin.
  6. Do not talk, scroll, or watch the display. Close your eyes and breathe slowly while the cuff inflates.
  7. Take at least two readings, 1 or 2 minutes apart, at the same time each day.
  8. Log every reading, including high ones. Do not re-measure until you get a number you like. Your doctor needs the average across days.
  9. Use a validated upper-arm monitor. The NHLBI advises an approved home device.

For a full home routine, see our home blood pressure monitoring guide. If your numbers jump around even when you follow these steps, read why blood pressure varies between readings.

One safety rule overrides the checklist. The NHLBI defines a hypertensive crisis as higher than 180 systolic or higher than 120 diastolic. If you get a reading in that range, rest and recheck. If it stays that high, or you have chest pain, trouble breathing, weakness, or trouble speaking, get emergency care. Do not assume it is only anxiety.

The bottom line

Anxiety raises blood pressure in the moment, and that rise is real. It does not by itself mean you have hypertension. Long-term studies show an association between anxiety and later hypertension, but they disagree on the size and direction of the link. Diagnose with calm, repeated readings on different days, not with a number taken mid-panic. Treat anxiety on its own merits, because it affects heart health through sleep, habits, and stress, whatever its exact effect on your cuff.

Frequently Asked Questions

How high can blood pressure go during a panic attack?

It varies from person to person, and there is no single number that applies to everyone. The rise is driven by the fight-or-flight response and usually fades as the panic passes. A reading taken mid-attack reflects that moment, not your resting blood pressure. If a reading stays above 180/120 after you rest, seek medical care.

Should I take blood pressure medicine for anxiety-related spikes?

Blood pressure medicine is prescribed based on your average resting pressure and overall heart risk, not on short spikes. If your calm home readings are normal, medicine for spikes alone is usually not the answer. Talk with your doctor about both your readings and your anxiety, since each may need its own plan.

Does treating anxiety lower blood pressure?

Treating anxiety can help with habits that affect blood pressure, such as sleep, alcohol use, smoking, and exercise. A 2021 American Heart Association statement said most data suggest that improving psychological health can benefit heart health. It is not a substitute for blood pressure treatment if your resting readings are high.

Why is my blood pressure normal at home but high at the clinic?

This pattern is called white coat hypertension, and nervousness about the visit is a common cause. The CDC says up to 1 in 3 people with a high office reading may read normal elsewhere. Bring a log of home readings to your appointment so your provider can compare them.

Can checking my blood pressure too often make me anxious?

Yes, for some people repeated checking becomes a source of worry. A set schedule, such as two readings at the same time each day, gives your doctor useful data without constant checking. Log every reading and avoid re-measuring to chase a better number.
