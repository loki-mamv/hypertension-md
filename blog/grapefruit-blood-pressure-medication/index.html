Can You Eat Grapefruit While Taking Blood Pressure Medication?

October 19, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: It depends on the drug. Grapefruit can raise blood levels of some calcium channel blockers, such as felodipine, nifedipine, and nisoldipine, and their labels warn against it. It has little or no effect on amlodipine, and common ACE inhibitors and thiazide diuretics are not broken down by the enzyme grapefruit blocks. Check your own medicine with your pharmacist.

Which blood pressure drugs interact with grapefruit?

The problem drugs sit mostly in one class: the dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. The FDA lists nifedipine (Procardia and Adalat CC) as an example of a blood pressure drug that grapefruit juice can affect. The FDA also requires some drug labels to warn against grapefruit and grapefruit juice.

Here is what the FDA-approved labels say:

  • Felodipine. The felodipine extended-release label says grapefruit juice raised the drug's exposure (AUC) and peak level more than 2-fold. It warns that CYP3A4 inhibitors, grapefruit juice among them, can lead to lower blood pressure and a faster heart rate.
  • Nifedipine. The Procardia XL label reports that grapefruit juice about doubled nifedipine levels. It states that grapefruit and grapefruit juice should be avoided while taking nifedipine.
  • Nisoldipine. The Sular label reports a mean 3-fold rise in peak level, up to about 7-fold in some people. It says grapefruit products should be avoided before and after dosing.
  • Verapamil. This is a different type of calcium channel blocker. Its extended-release label says grapefruit juice may significantly increase verapamil levels, with AUC rises of 28% to 36% in nine volunteers.

More drug in the blood can mean a stronger effect than intended. For a blood pressure drug, that can mean a faster heart rate, headache, or a pressure that drops too low. Our guide to what blood pressure is too low explains the warning signs.

Which blood pressure drugs are not affected?

Amlodipine is the calcium channel blocker most people take, and it is the big exception in its class. The Norvasc label states that grapefruit juice has no impact on exposure to amlodipine. A small 1996 study in 12 healthy men found a glass of grapefruit juice raised amlodipine levels by about 15%, with no significant change in blood pressure or heart rate. The authors still noted that people vary, and that larger amounts of juice might matter more.

Many other first-line drugs do not depend on intestinal CYP3A4 at all:

  • Lisinopril. The lisinopril label says the drug does not undergo metabolism and is excreted unchanged in the urine.
  • Hydrochlorothiazide. The hydrochlorothiazide label says it is not metabolized and is cleared by the kidneys.

A drug that the body does not break down through CYP3A4 has little for grapefruit to block. Still, "class" is not a guarantee. Combination pills can pair a safe drug with an affected one. Our comparison of blood pressure medications lists the main classes and common names.

How does grapefruit change drug levels?

Many drugs are partly broken down in the wall of the small intestine before they reach the blood. An enzyme called CYP3A4 does much of that work. According to the FDA, grapefruit juice blocks intestinal CYP3A4, so more of the drug enters the blood and stays in the body longer.

The compounds behind this are furanocoumarins, such as bergamottin and 6',7'-dihydroxybergamottin. A 2001 study in Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics called them likely "marker substances" for the interaction. It concluded the main mechanism is probably inactivation of intestinal CYP3A4.

That is also why the effect varies so much. The FDA notes that the amount of CYP3A4 in the intestine differs from person to person. Two people on the same drug can react very differently to the same glass of juice.

Grapefruit can also act the opposite way on a few drugs. The FDA explains that it can block drug transporters and lower the amount of some medicines, such as fexofenadine. That effect is not a major issue for the blood pressure drugs above.

How long does the effect of one glass last?

Spacing your juice and your pill apart by a few hours does not solve the problem. In a 1995 study of nine healthy men on felodipine, about 200 mL of grapefruit juice raised peak felodipine levels by 32% to 99%. That held even when the juice came 24 hours before the pill. The effect on total exposure was still significant when the juice was taken up to 10 hours before. The effect got smaller as the gap grew longer.

A single glass is enough to cause the full effect. A 1998 study by the same group gave 12 volunteers felodipine with grapefruit juice daily for 14 days. The rise in drug levels on day 1 was about the same as on day 14. The authors concluded the interaction was fully developed after the first glass.

The likely reason is that the juice inactivates the enzyme rather than just slowing it. Since the effect can last about a day, the practical choice for affected drugs is usually to skip grapefruit, not to time it.

What about oranges, pomelos, and other citrus?

Ordinary sweet oranges are not the concern. The felodipine label says orange juice does not appear to change how the drug works, and the 2001 study used common orange juice as its safe control.

Seville (bitter) oranges are different. In that Seville orange study, Seville orange juice raised felodipine exposure by 76%, close to the 93% rise from diluted grapefruit juice. Seville oranges are common in marmalade, so read labels if you take an affected drug.

Pomelos and some grapefruit hybrids are close relatives of grapefruit. The FDA advises asking your pharmacist what other fruits or juices may affect your drug in a similar way. Treat any grapefruit-like citrus with the same caution until you have an answer.

What should you do if you take an affected drug?

  1. Find out your exact drug. Read the patient leaflet or the label that came with your prescription. Look for a grapefruit warning.
  2. Ask your pharmacist. The FDA tells people to ask a provider or pharmacist whether their drug is affected and how much grapefruit, if any, is safe.
  3. Do not stop or switch on your own. Stopping a blood pressure drug can let pressure climb. If you are tempted to skip a pill, read what to do after a missed dose first.
  4. Watch for signs of too much drug. The felodipine label links higher levels to lower blood pressure and a faster heart rate, and headache was more common with juice in one study. If you check your pressure at home, note any sudden drops.

If you also take supplements with your blood pressure pills, supplements.md covers what to check for in those products.

The bottom line

Grapefruit is a real problem for a handful of blood pressure drugs, mainly felodipine, nifedipine, nisoldipine, and to a lesser extent verapamil. It has little or no effect on amlodipine, lisinopril, or hydrochlorothiazide. One glass can affect drug levels for about a day, so timing your juice does not help. Seville oranges act like grapefruit, while ordinary oranges do not. Your pharmacist can tell you in a minute whether your exact pill is on the list.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to eat grapefruit if I take amlodipine?

The Norvasc label states that grapefruit juice has no impact on amlodipine exposure. A small study found only about a 15% rise in drug levels with no significant change in blood pressure. Ask your pharmacist to confirm, especially if you take a combination pill.

Does a small amount of grapefruit matter?

For affected drugs such as felodipine, studies used a single glass of about 200 to 250 mL and still found large rises in drug levels. The FDA says the severity depends on the person, the drug, and how much juice you drink. Your pharmacist can tell you whether any amount is safe with your medicine.

Can I take my pill in the morning and have grapefruit at night?

For felodipine, grapefruit juice raised drug levels even when it was taken 24 hours before the pill. That means spacing the two apart by a few hours does not remove the interaction. For affected drugs, avoiding grapefruit is the usual advice on the label.

Is orange juice a safe swap for grapefruit juice?

Regular sweet orange juice did not change felodipine levels in studies and is listed as having no effect on the felodipine label. Seville (bitter) orange juice did raise felodipine levels, by 76% in one study. Check marmalade and juice blends for Seville orange.

Do losartan or other ARBs interact with grapefruit?

The losartan label we checked has no grapefruit warning. Most ARBs are not on the FDA's examples of grapefruit-sensitive drugs. Because labels differ by drug, confirm your specific ARB with a pharmacist.
