Can High Blood Pressure Cause Nosebleeds?

November 22, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: High blood pressure is linked to nosebleeds, but it is not proven to cause them. A 2017 meta-analysis of 10 studies found people with hypertension had about 1.5 times the odds of a nosebleed. The bigger effect may be on severity: hypertensive patients more often need emergency care and deeper nasal packing.

What do studies on blood pressure and nosebleeds actually show?

Doctors have argued about this question for decades. The research now points to a real link, with clear limits on what it proves.

  • Risk of any nosebleed. A systematic review and meta-analysis in Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery screened 2,768 studies and kept 10. Hypertension raised the odds of epistaxis (the medical word for nosebleed), with an odds ratio of 1.53. The authors said this association does not prove cause.
  • A large national cohort. A 2020 study in JAMA Otolaryngology compared 35,749 Korean adults treated for hypertension with 35,749 matched adults without it. Nosebleeds that needed a hospital visit occurred at 32.97 per 10,000 people in the hypertension group and 22.76 per 10,000 in the comparison group. That is about 45% more often.
  • Recurrence. In the same Korean study, repeat nosebleeds were slightly more common with hypertension (1.96 vs 1.59 per 10,000), but the difference was not statistically significant.
  • Studies that found no link. A 2024 Italian emergency department study of 102 nosebleed patients and 126 controls found no significant tie between hypertension and nosebleeds. Blood thinners were the main independent risk factor.

So the evidence is mixed in places, but the balance favors a modest association. It does not show that a high reading on a given day starts a bleed.

Does high blood pressure make nosebleeds worse or harder to stop?

This is where the data look strongest. Most nosebleeds start in the front of the nose (anterior bleeds) and stop with pressure. Posterior bleeds come from deeper vessels, bleed more heavily, and often need a doctor.

In the Korean cohort, people with hypertension who had a nosebleed were 2.69 times as likely to go to the emergency department. They were also 4.58 times as likely to need posterior nasal packing, a sign of a deeper or harder bleed. The confidence range on the packing figure was wide, so treat the exact number with caution.

Severity of hypertension did not seem to drive how often bleeds happen. A small one-year follow-up of 36 hypertensive patients found about 8 nosebleeds per year in each hypertension stage, with no real difference between stages.

Is the high reading the cause, or a result of the nosebleed?

A nosebleed is frightening. Fear, pain, and a busy emergency room all push blood pressure up. That makes a high reading during a bleed hard to read.

A 2014 systematic review of nine studies found that most studies recorded higher pressure at the time of a nosebleed. The authors said this cannot prove cause, because of stress and the white coat effect. They also noted that the high reading may reveal hypertension that was already there.

Two findings support the stress view. In the Italian study, 73% of nosebleed patients had pressure over 140/90 on arrival, but only 26% did 30 minutes later. In the Brazilian follow-up, systolic pressure averaged 157 during nosebleeds and 154 on routine weekly checks, which was not a meaningful difference.

The 2020 AAO-HNS clinical practice guideline on nosebleed treats hypertension as a related condition to manage, not a proven cause. Its lead author told ENTtoday that the guideline covers the lack of evidence for a direct causal link. If your pressure is high during a bleed, recheck it once you are calm. When is a nosebleed with a very high reading an emergency?

The American Heart Association defines a hypertensive crisis as a reading higher than 180/120 mm Hg. Its advice is to wait at least 1 minute and measure again. If the reading is still that high, check for warning signs.

Call 911 if your pressure is above 180/120 and you have any of these:

  • Chest pain or back pain
  • Shortness of breath
  • Numbness or weakness
  • A change in vision or difficulty speaking

The AHA page does not list nosebleeds among these signs. A nosebleed alone with a high reading is not, by that definition, a crisis. But a bleed that will not stop is its own emergency. If the reading stays above 180/120 without symptoms, the AHA says to contact your health care professional as soon as possible. Our page on what to do at 180 or higher walks through those steps.

What else causes nosebleeds besides blood pressure?

Most nosebleeds have a local cause. MedlinePlus lists irritation from allergies, colds, sneezing, or sinus problems. Dry air, hard nose blowing, injury, and chemical irritants are also common triggers.

Medicines matter a lot. MedlinePlus says blood thinners such as warfarin, clopidogrel (Plavix), and aspirin may cause or worsen nosebleeds. Many people with hypertension also take aspirin or an anticoagulant for heart or stroke protection, which can blur the link with blood pressure. In the 2024 study, anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy was the main independent risk factor.

The AAO-HNS guideline tells clinicians to record factors that raise bleeding risk. These include a personal or family history of bleeding disorders, blood thinner use, and intranasal drug use. Repeated bleeds on both sides, or a family history of them, can point to hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, an inherited blood vessel disorder. How do you stop a nosebleed, step by step?

This checklist combines MedlinePlus home care advice with the AAO-HNS guideline, which recommends firm, sustained pressure on the lower third of the nose for 5 minutes or longer.

  1. Sit down and lean forward, so you do not swallow blood. Breathe through your mouth.
  2. Pinch the soft, lower part of your nose firmly, not the bony bridge.
  3. Hold the pressure for a full 10 minutes without letting go to check.
  4. Put a cold compress on the bridge of your nose if it helps.
  5. Do not pack your nose with gauze. Do not sniff or blow your nose for several hours after.
  6. Once the bleeding stops and you are calm, recheck your blood pressure with a home cuff.
  7. Get emergency care if bleeding lasts more than 20 minutes, follows a head injury, may involve a broken nose, or you take blood thinners.
  8. Call your provider if nosebleeds are frequent or happen without a cold or minor irritation.

To prevent repeat bleeds, MedlinePlus suggests a cool home, a vaporizer, saline nasal spray, and a water-based gel to keep the nasal lining moist, especially in winter. If your nosebleeds keep coming back, track your pressure at home on calm days. Our guide to home blood pressure monitoring explains how to get accurate readings.

The bottom line

High blood pressure is linked to more frequent and more severe nosebleeds, but studies have not shown it causes them. A high reading during a bleed is often stress, so recheck once you are calm. Pinch the soft part of your nose for 10 minutes while leaning forward. Seek emergency care for bleeding past 20 minutes, a head injury, blood thinner use, or a reading above 180/120 with chest pain, breathlessness, weakness, or vision or speech changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I take my blood pressure during a nosebleed?

Stop the bleeding first. Pain and fear raise blood pressure, so a reading taken mid-bleed is often higher than your usual level. Recheck once you are calm, and repeat it on later days to see your true baseline.

Can blood pressure medicine cause nosebleeds?

The medicines MedlinePlus links to nosebleeds are blood thinners. Blood thinners such as aspirin, warfarin, and clopidogrel can cause or worsen them. Do not stop any prescribed medicine on your own; ask your doctor if bleeds are frequent.

Are frequent nosebleeds a sign of undiagnosed hypertension?

They can be one reason to get your blood pressure checked, and MedlinePlus notes repeated nosebleeds may be a symptom of high blood pressure. Dry air, colds, allergies, and hard nose blowing are common causes too. A few calm, at-home readings will tell you more than one reading taken during a bleed.

Should I tilt my head back to stop a nosebleed?

No. Lean forward instead so blood does not run down your throat. Pinch the soft part of your nose for a full 10 minutes without checking.

Is a nosebleed a sign of a stroke?

A nosebleed is not one of the AHA warning signs of a hypertensive crisis. Numbness, weakness, vision changes, or trouble speaking with a reading above 180/120 are the signs that need a 911 call. Call 911 for those symptoms whether or not your nose is bleeding.
