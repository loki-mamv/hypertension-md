How Long Does Blood Pressure Medication Take to Work?

October 9, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Most blood pressure medicines start to work within hours of the first dose. The full effect of a dose takes longer, usually 1 to 6 weeks, and it depends on the drug. A review of 18 trials found that a new medicine reaches half of its maximum effect in about 1 week. Doctors usually recheck about a month after a start or a change.

How fast does the first dose of blood pressure medication work?

The first dose works within hours for most common drugs. FDA labels give these times:

  • Lisinopril (ACE inhibitor): the lisinopril label says the effect started 1 hour after a dose in most patients studied. The peak drop in blood pressure came by 6 hours.
  • Valsartan (ARB): the valsartan label says the effect starts at about 2 hours after a single dose. The maximum drop comes within 6 hours.
  • Hydrochlorothiazide (thiazide diuretic): the hydrochlorothiazide label says action starts within 2 hours. The peak effect is at about 4 hours, and activity lasts up to 24 hours.
  • Chlorthalidone (thiazide-type diuretic): the chlorthalidone label says the diuretic effect occurs in about 2.6 hours and continues for up to 72 hours.

Amlodipine is different. The amlodipine label describes a "gradual onset of effect". The drug binds to its target slowly and leaves it slowly.

A first-dose effect is not the final result. One dose wears off. The lisinopril label says the effect at 24 hours was substantially smaller than the effect at 6 hours, at all doses studied. A stable result needs days or weeks of daily doses.

How long until blood pressure medication reaches its full effect?

The full effect of a dose takes from about 1 week to 6 weeks. The labels give different times for different drugs:

  • Metoprolol succinate (beta-blocker): the label says the maximum effect of a dose is generally apparent after 1 week.
  • Amlodipine (calcium channel blocker): the label says blood levels become steady after 7 to 8 days of daily doses.
  • Valsartan: the label says the drop is substantially present within 2 weeks. The maximum drop generally comes after 4 weeks.
  • Losartan (ARB): the losartan label says the effect is substantially present within 1 week. In some studies the maximum effect came in 3 to 6 weeks.

The lisinopril, hydrochlorothiazide and chlorthalidone labels we read give no number of weeks for the full effect. The hydrochlorothiazide label does explain why the effect changes with time. At first, thiazides probably lower pressure because they reduce blood volume. With long-term use, blood volume returns toward normal and the blood vessels resist flow less.

Do some blood pressure drug classes work faster than others?

The labels suggest some differences, but pooled trial data show less difference than you might expect. A 2011 systematic review in the journal Heart modelled the response in 18 trials with 4,168 patients. Each trial started one medicine in people who took no other blood pressure drug.

The review found these results:

  • A medicine reached 50% of its maximum estimated effect in about 1 week. The figure was 0.91 weeks for systolic pressure and 0.95 weeks for diastolic pressure.
  • Models that included drug class did not fit the data better. The time course looked similar across classes.
  • Trials with a stepped dose schedule took longer to reach the halfway point: 1.2 weeks compared with 0.7 weeks for systolic pressure.

The authors concluded that doctors can estimate the maximum effect of a medicine early after the start. In plain terms, the first one to two weeks tell you a lot. The last part of the drop comes more slowly.

When will your doctor recheck your blood pressure and adjust the dose?

The usual interval is about a month. An NHLBI guideline report (JNC 7) says most patients should return at approximately monthly intervals for follow-up and adjustment, until they reach the goal. It says people with stage 2 hypertension or other conditions need more frequent visits. After blood pressure is stable at the goal, visits can usually be 3 to 6 months apart.

That report is from 2003. The current U.S. guideline is the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline. We could not load its full text, so we do not quote its follow-up schedule here.

The labels also set a pace for dose changes. The amlodipine label says to wait 7 to 14 days between dose steps in general. The metoprolol succinate label says to adjust the dose at weekly or longer intervals. This is why a doctor does not judge a dose after two days.

Some people start with two medicines. The American Heart Association summary of the 2025 guideline says the guideline recommends two medicines at the start for blood pressure of 140/90 mm Hg or higher. It prefers one combination pill. The summary also says many people need more than one medicine to get below 130/80 mm Hg.

What should you do if your numbers do not move?

First, give the medicine the time that its label describes. Then look at the common reasons for a poor result before you decide that the drug has failed.

  • Check your measurements. Poor technique can give false numbers. Follow the steps in our guide to home blood pressure monitoring. Take a log of readings to your visit.
  • Take every dose. The effect of one dose fades by the next day. Missed doses give uneven numbers.
  • Check your other medicines. Some pain relievers and decongestants raise blood pressure. See our list of OTC medicines that raise blood pressure.
  • Keep your lifestyle changes. NHLBI says to keep healthy lifestyle changes while you take these medicines. Salt intake is a large part of this. Our sibling site calories.md covers food and nutrition numbers.
  • Tell your doctor. NHLBI says your provider may change the dose or give you a new medicine.

Some people stay above their goal on three drug classes at full doses. Doctors call this resistant hypertension, and it needs a separate workup.

Is it safe to stop or change the dose yourself if it does not seem to work?

No. MedlinePlus says never change the dose or stop a medicine on your own. Two reasons stand out.

First, you cannot feel the result. MedlinePlus says high blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so the numbers are the only test. A medicine can work well and you will feel no different.

Second, some drugs are not safe to stop suddenly. The metoprolol succinate label warns that sudden stops of some beta-blockers have led to worse chest pain and, in some cases, heart attack. It says to reduce the dose gradually over 1 to 2 weeks. It also says to avoid a sudden stop in people treated only for hypertension, because heart disease can be present and not yet found.

Other drugs act differently. The losartan label reports no apparent rebound effect after a sudden stop. The lisinopril and valsartan labels say a sudden stop has not been linked to a rapid rise in blood pressure. Your blood pressure will still return toward its old level without treatment. The decision belongs with your doctor.

Low readings also need a call. The losartan patient information says low blood pressure can make you feel faint or dizzy, and it says to lie down if this happens.

The bottom line

Blood pressure medicine starts to work within hours, and about half of the final drop arrives in the first week. The full effect of a dose takes 1 to 6 weeks, based on the drug. Expect a recheck about a month after a start or a dose change. Measure at home, write the numbers down and take every dose. If the numbers do not move, tell your doctor and do not change the dose yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does lisinopril lower blood pressure on the first day?

Yes, in most people. The FDA label says the effect started 1 hour after a dose in most patients studied, and the peak drop came by 6 hours. The effect at 24 hours was substantially smaller than at 6 hours, so one dose does not show the final result.

How long does losartan take to reach its full effect?

The FDA label says the effect of losartan is substantially present within 1 week. In some studies the maximum effect occurred in 3 to 6 weeks. Your doctor will usually wait for that period before a decision about the dose.

Why does amlodipine seem slow to work?

The FDA label describes a gradual onset of effect, because the drug binds to its target slowly. Blood levels become steady after 7 to 8 days of daily doses. The label says to wait 7 to 14 days between dose steps in general.

Will I feel different when my blood pressure medicine starts to work?

Usually not. High blood pressure usually has no symptoms, so a lower number does not change how you feel. Home readings are the reliable test. Tell your doctor if you feel faint or dizzy, because that can be a sign of low blood pressure.

How often should I check my blood pressure after a new prescription?

Ask your doctor for a schedule. Home readings at the same times each day give a clear record of the trend. Take the log to your follow-up visit, which is often about a month after the start.
