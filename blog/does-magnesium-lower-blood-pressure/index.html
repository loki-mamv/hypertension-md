Does Magnesium Lower Blood Pressure?

November 9, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes, but only a little for most people. A 2025 meta-analysis in Hypertension of 38 randomized trials found magnesium lowered systolic pressure by about 2.8 mm Hg and diastolic by about 2.1 mm Hg. Larger drops showed up in people with low magnesium or treated hypertension. It does not replace medication.

What do randomized trials show about magnesium and blood pressure?

Magnesium has been tested in placebo-controlled trials for decades. Three pooled analyses give a consistent picture: a real effect, but a small one.

  • 2025, 38 trials, 2,709 people. The Argeros meta-analysis included trials of at least 4 weeks. The median dose was 365 mg of elemental magnesium for a median of 12 weeks. Systolic pressure fell 2.81 mm Hg and diastolic 2.05 mm Hg compared with placebo.
  • 2016, 34 trials, 2,028 people. A double-blind trial meta-analysis found a median dose of 368 mg a day for 3 months lowered systolic pressure by 2.00 mm Hg and diastolic by 1.78 mm Hg.
  • 2012, 22 trials, 1,173 people. An earlier pooled analysis found drops of 3 to 4 mm Hg systolic and 2 to 3 mm Hg diastolic, with larger effects above 370 mg a day.

The 2025 authors flagged high heterogeneity, meaning the trials varied a lot in design and results. They found no clear dose-response link (all P values 0.20 or higher). The 2016 group, by contrast, reported that 300 mg a day or 1 month was enough to raise blood magnesium and lower pressure. Treat the exact dose as unsettled.

For one person, a 2 to 3 mm Hg change is smaller than the normal swing between two readings taken minutes apart. Our guide on why blood pressure varies between readings explains that noise. You would not be able to see the effect of magnesium on a home monitor from day to day.

The 2025 meta-analysis also found that in people with normal blood pressure, the change did not reach statistical significance. So magnesium is not a tool to push a normal reading lower. It is a possible small add-on for some people with high readings, decided with a clinician.

Who seems to benefit most?

Subgroup results point to three groups:

  • People with low blood magnesium (hypomagnesemia). In the 2025 analysis, this group saw systolic drops of 5.97 mm Hg and diastolic drops of 4.75 mm Hg.
  • People with hypertension already on blood pressure medicine. This subgroup saw systolic drops of 7.68 mm Hg and diastolic drops of 2.96 mm Hg.
  • People with insulin resistance, prediabetes, or chronic disease. A 2017 meta-analysis of 11 trials and 543 people found a mean fall of 4.18 mm Hg systolic and 2.27 mm Hg diastolic. Trial doses ran from 365 to 450 mg a day.

Subgroup findings are less certain than the main result. They suggest a pattern: magnesium helps most when there is a shortfall to correct. MedlinePlus lists who is more likely to run low: people with type 2 diabetes, older adults, people who misuse alcohol, and people with gut disease or surgery that limits absorption. Some medicines can also lower magnesium, covered below.

Which foods supply magnesium?

MedlinePlus says most dietary magnesium comes from dark green, leafy vegetables. Other good sources include:

  • Nuts such as almonds and cashews, and seeds
  • Peas and beans (legumes)
  • Soy foods such as tofu
  • Whole grains such as brown rice and millet
  • Fruits such as bananas, dried apricots, and avocados
  • Milk

The adult Recommended Dietary Allowance is 400 to 420 mg a day for men and 310 to 320 mg for women, depending on age. MedlinePlus notes that taking magnesium supplements for high blood pressure is not currently advised. A food-first pattern such as the DASH diet supplies magnesium along with potassium and fiber. For the potassium side of that pattern, see our guide to potassium and salt substitutes.

How much supplemental magnesium is too much?

The US Institute of Medicine (now the National Academies) set a Tolerable Upper Intake Level of 350 mg a day for adults. As a 2023 review in Advances in Nutrition explains, that limit covers supplements and medicines only, not magnesium in food. Diarrhea was the limiting effect.

That review argues the limit should be re-examined, since several trials used more without extra diarrhea. The official limit has not changed, and many of the blood pressure trials used doses near or above it. That is one more reason to agree on any supplement with your clinician first. For general guidance on choosing supplements, our sister site supplements.md covers labels and quality.

What about kidney disease and drug interactions?

Kidneys. Healthy kidneys remove extra magnesium. MedlinePlus says side effects from high intake are not common, except in people with significantly reduced kidney function. A magnesium oxide supplement label tells people with kidney disease to consult a physician first and warns it may have a laxative effect. Since high blood pressure is a common cause of kidney damage, this matters for many readers.

Interactions named on drug labels:

  • Some antibiotics. The ciprofloxacin label says to take it at least 2 hours before or 6 hours after magnesium-containing products. Magnesium antacids can cut its absorption by as much as 90%.
  • Osteoporosis drugs. The alendronate label says oral products with multivalent cations, which includes magnesium, interfere with its absorption.
  • Acid reducers. The omeprazole label says low magnesium has been reported rarely with proton pump inhibitor use of at least three months, usually after a year. It names diuretics as drugs that can also lower magnesium, and suggests checking levels in people who take them.

Diuretics are a common blood pressure medicine class. Our blood pressure medications compared guide covers which ones may need lab monitoring.

What should you ask your clinician before trying magnesium?

Bring these questions to your next visit or pharmacy check. Each one comes from the trial findings or label warnings above.

  1. Is my kidney function normal? Ask about your most recent kidney blood test.
  2. Should my blood magnesium be checked? The trial data suggest people with low levels gain the most.
  3. Do any of my medicines lower magnesium? Name any diuretic or long-term acid reducer you take.
  4. Will a supplement interfere with my other drugs? List antibiotics, osteoporosis drugs, and any antacids or laxatives that already contain magnesium.
  5. Can I meet my needs from food instead? Ask whether diet changes alone are enough.
  6. How will we judge whether it helped? Agree on a plan of home readings before and after, since a 2 to 3 mm Hg change is easy to miss.
  7. Does this change my treatment plan? Confirm that you keep taking your prescribed medicine as directed.

The bottom line

Magnesium lowers blood pressure, but modestly: about 2 to 3 mm Hg on average across dozens of trials. People with low magnesium, insulin resistance, or treated hypertension may see more. Food sources come first. Supplements carry a 350 mg adult upper limit, kidney cautions, and real drug interactions, so check with your clinician before starting one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can magnesium replace my blood pressure medication?

No. The average drop in trials was about 2 to 3 mm Hg, far less than most prescription medicines provide. Keep taking your prescribed medicine and discuss any supplement with your clinician.

How long did magnesium take to work in studies?

The 2025 meta-analysis included trials lasting at least 4 weeks, with a median of 12 weeks. A 2016 analysis reported that about 1 month was enough to raise blood magnesium and lower pressure. Results varied a lot between trials.

Does magnesium lower blood pressure if my readings are already normal?

Probably not by a meaningful amount. In the 2025 meta-analysis, the change in people with normal blood pressure did not reach statistical significance.

Is magnesium from food limited by the 350 mg upper limit?

No. The 350 mg adult upper limit set by the Institute of Medicine applies to supplements and medicines only, not to magnesium naturally present in food. Healthy kidneys remove extra magnesium from food.

Is magnesium safe if I have kidney disease?

Ask your doctor first. MedlinePlus says side effects from high magnesium intake mainly occur in people with significantly reduced kidney function, and supplement labels tell people with kidney disease to consult a physician.
